Readouts, Not Decks

One session in which student teams stop making slides and start making research that survives being forwarded — a scrolling page a partner org can open on a phone, read with nobody there to explain it, and send to someone who was never in the room. The session builds the argument. The page is built afterward.

A deck assumes a room. Almost nothing about a partner engagement survives one.

The premise

A deck is a performance aid. It needs a presenter, a room, and forty minutes of attention. What actually happens to it is that it gets emailed, forwarded to someone who wasn't there, opened on a phone, and read with nobody around to say the sentence that made slide 14 make sense.

A readout is built for that condition instead. It's read alone, in silence, out of order, by someone who might give it ninety seconds. Three things follow that a deck lets you avoid:

  • The argument has to be on the page. You can't narrate the gap between two charts. If the claim isn't written down, it doesn't exist.
  • Every number carries its own conditions. No presenter to say "small sample, obviously." The n goes on the chart or the chart is dishonest.
  • It outlives the meeting. A link reaches the night-shift supervisor who was never going to be in the room. For this course that's the difference between a culture recommendation reaching the people it describes and staying with the executives who commissioned it.

There's also a plain professional argument. This is what serious research communication looks like now. A student who leaves able to make one has a transferable skill; a student who leaves able to make a nice deck has the skill everyone already has.

What students leave able to do

The capability, not the coverage

Take a body of research and turn it into an argument that holds up when nobody is there to defend it. The HTML is the forcing function, not the skill. What gets practised is deciding what you're claiming, what evidence sits under each claim, what you chose to measure and what that choice reveals about what you think matters, and what you're willing to admit you might have wrong.

ArtifactsStoriesMetrics

The readout is itself a durable culture artifact; it is a narrative claim about an organization made to circulate inside it; and what you choose to chart is a statement about what counts — which is the whole lesson of the transmission section in the specimen.

They get shared evidence, from three sources, and it is not their partner's

The evidence pack

Three reasons the workshop runs on a common dataset rather than each team's own discovery material:

  • Identical evidence makes craft visible. Six readings of the same rows, and the only difference between a good one and a bad one is the argument. Nowhere to hide behind "we had better interviews."
  • Teams arrive uneven. In week 6 one team has twenty interviews and another has four and a rescheduled site visit. Their own data means half the room spends the session on cleanup.
  • Publishing partner data is a consent problem. Interview quotes about what's broken at a real medical center shouldn't go on a URL as a skills exercise. Taking it off the table lets you raise it deliberately in week 7, where it belongs.

Not a dataset — three sources that disagree

The specimens aren't built on a dataset. They're built on triangulation: the Work Trend Index puts survey responses beside Microsoft 365 telemetry, and the authority comes from the two diverging in interesting places. That maps onto this course's own taxonomy — the invisible forces are what people tell you, the visible ones are what the organization records.

The survey — 148 recent hires

Cedar Ridge Health, a fictional regional health system. Role, shift, work mode, hire quarter, eleven self-report measures about the first month.

The systems data — 131 of those people

What the organization logged: scheduling, paging directory, the learning platform, and the AI assistant. Joins on respondent ID. The 17 who don't match are per-diem and agency staff on a different system.

The interviews — 22 coded excerpts

Eighteen new hires and four managers, tagged by role and tenure. Never by name.

What's engineered into it

A field list can't teach anything. These findings were built into how the numbers were generated:

It's synthetic and says so on its face. Say that out loud in class too — labelling a synthetic dataset is itself part of research honesty.

Pre-work

Assigned the session before · two minutes of class time · now load-bearing

Read three published readouts — the two Microsoft WorkLab pieces and the Anthropic Institute one — at least fifteen minutes each. Assign which one each student covers, so the room has all three in it. Fill the teardown sheet on paper. Nothing else, and explicitly: don't start building. With only one session this is not optional homework — the in-class teardown block is gone, and the contract at 4:40 is harvested straight off these sheets.

1 · What is this piece claiming? One sentence, your words. 2 · Where is that sentence actually written down? Quote it. 3 · Count the interactive elements. For each, what question does it answer that a static image couldn't? 4 · How many kinds of evidence is it using? Find two placed side by side. Do they agree? 5 · Pick a chart. What does it tell you about its own sample and limits? 6 · Where did you stop reading, honestly? 7 · What would be lost if this were a deck? What would be gained?

The session

110 minutes · one block · laptops closed until 5:45

Four things have to happen and there is time for all four, because the build is not one of them. Students leave with an argument they have already had cold-read by strangers, and a demonstrated method for turning it into a page. The page itself they finish on their own time, which is the part an assistant genuinely helps with.

4:3010 min

Cold read, on ourselves

Don't explain the workshop. Put last year's best student deck on screen and say only: "ninety seconds, read this the way you'd read it if it landed in your inbox." Scroll it. Say nothing — your instinct to narrate the slide that needs narrating is the entire point.

Close it. "In one sentence, what did that deck claim?" Read four answers aloud. They won't match.

"That deck is good. The people who made it knew exactly what it claimed. The claim was in the room, in their voices, and it did not survive the email. For the rest of the quarter we are making things that survive the email."

Get permission for the deck beforehand, and use a good one — the demonstration collapses if anyone can dismiss it as a bad deck.

4:4010 min

Harvest the contract

Straight from the pre-work sheets, at the wall, in their words. No pair block — the teardown already happened at home, which is why the pre-work is now load-bearing rather than optional.

They reliably produce: the argument has to be written down, you need the sample size, don't make me scroll to find the point. They reliably miss three, so fish: "how many interactive things were in the Anthropic piece?" → restraint. "What happens when you open one of these on a phone in a corridor?" "Where's the part that tells you why they might be wrong?" → limits.

Ten minutes is enough because you are collecting, not teaching. Put the nine-item contract up beside their list and leave both there.

4:5025 min

The claim spine — no screens

Hand out the evidence packet on paper and index cards. Five to seven claims, one per card, each a full sentence with a verb, in the order you'd want them read. No charts, no laptops, no formatting.

Circulate for cards with no verb. "AI and onboarding." "The night shift problem." Point and say "that's a topic, give me the sentence." You'll say it fifteen times in twenty-five minutes. That is the job.

In class they get the survey and the interviews only. The systems file comes later, and deliberately so — see the homework brief.

5:1520 min

The silent spine gallery

Card stacks laid out on tables around the room, cold-read cards beside each. Nobody stands at their own. Four rotations, four minutes each, in silence. Read a stranger's spine, leave a card, move.

1 · In one sentence, what is this readout claiming? 2 · Which claim is doing the least work? 3 · Where does the argument jump?

This is the gallery, moved onto the spines — and it works better here than on finished pages, because there is no chart, no typography and no colour to hide a weak argument behind. Twelve index cards either hold as a case or they don't.

Your job is to be a bouncer. Authors drift toward their own tables and people start talking. Move them quietly. The silence is the condition you're simulating.

5:3510 min

Read your cards, then cut one

Teams read their stack in silence for three minutes, then say one line out loud: "Readers thought we were claiming ___. We meant ___." That gap is the critique, and the cards are more credible to them than you are — resist adding.

Then every team physically removes one card. Cutting, not adding. Seven claims is a team that hasn't decided.

5:458 min

Live build

Laptops open. Build a section on the room screen, out loud, with an assistant. Not a polished demo — the point is watching you work, including the part where it goes wrong. Three things must happen:

1 · Say the claim as a sentence before typing anything. 2 · Paste the contract into the prompt, not a vague ask. 3 · Check one number against the source, find it wrong or unverifiable, and fix it. If the assistant gets everything right, check anyway and say out loud what you were looking for.

Use a claim no team is working on. This is the block that makes the homework feel possible rather than frightening, so demonstrate the whole loop, badly and fast, rather than one part of it well.

5:5317 min

Build your first section

Starter file open. One section: claim, one chart, one quote, one axis note. Nobody finishes and nobody is meant to — the purpose is that every team hits their first real obstacle while you are still in the room.

Circulate fast and shallow. Unblock, don't critique. The question stays "show me the sentence that says that."

Every team publishes before they leave, however unfinished. Getting the URL now means the homework is editing something that exists rather than starting something that doesn't.

6:1010 min

Close, and hand out the complication

The homework brief, below. Give it verbally as well as on paper, because the second data source needs a sentence of framing or teams will read it as extra work rather than as the point.

The one thing to hold

Around 4:58 a team will say they think better on a screen, or that they just need to check one number. Say no. Twenty-five minutes of writing sentences you cannot style is the only reliable way to get an argument before an aesthetic. It is the entire pedagogical bet, and with one session you have no second chance to recover it.

If the block is running hot, take the minutes from the build at 5:53, never from the spine or the gallery. A team that leaves with fifteen minutes of building and a tested argument is in better shape than one that leaves with a styled page and a spine nobody read.

The homework, and why the second data source arrives late

Due before the next session

Finish the readout against the contract and publish it to the URL you already have. Then the part that matters:

The complication

Handed out at 6:10, not at 4:50. Here is a second source — what the organization's systems actually logged for 131 of the 148 people you have been reading about. Scheduling, the paging directory, the learning platform, the assistant logs.

Your spine was written from what people said. This is what the systems recorded. Join it and answer one question in your readout: does it change your argument? If it does, say how. If it doesn't, say why not — and show the reader the comparison that let you decide.

Giving them everything up front would have been the obvious move and it is the worse one. Committing to an argument and then receiving evidence that complicates it is both the harder intellectual exercise and a truer description of how research actually goes. It also means the triangulation is something they perform rather than something they are handed.

What is waiting in there: the two sources agree on the week-one guide almost exactly, and then invert at the extremes — physicians report the lowest sense of knowing who to ask while contacting roughly twice as many colleagues as average, and Support Services are the mirror image. Contact volume is not access. No team will have that claim on a card, because it is in neither file alone.

And the question underneath it, which you raise when the readouts come back: two of those columns are paging metadata and shift records, collected to run schedules and now used to decide whether people feel like they belong. Nobody told the 131 people.

"Would you show this readout to the people it describes?"

Not looking for an answer. Looking for the recognition that choosing what to measure is itself a culture intervention — force eight, which they will have just performed on 131 people without discussing it first.

Support for the teams that need it

  • The starter file means nobody begins at zero, and they leave class having already used it once.
  • Name the checker role before they leave: one person on each team owns every number against its source file and every quote against its speaker ID. Nobody volunteers for this and everyone assumes someone else is doing it.
  • Pair a team with no technical confidence to a team with some — for the build only, never for the argument.

The readout contract

Nine constraints · on the board both sessions · graded against

  1. Five to seven claims, in orderEach a full sentence with a verb, set in the largest type on the page. A reader who scrolls past everything else and reads only these gets the whole argument.
  2. The claim sits in the heading, never the chart title"Two layers, two results" is a chart title. "The written layer transmits cleanly and the unwritten layer doesn't transmit at all" is a claim.
  3. Give away the finding in the first eighty wordsNobody is in the room to build suspense for.
  4. Every figure carries its conditionsThe n, the scale, where the bars start, and the thing that would let someone misread it. One line under every chart, no exceptions.
  5. Exactly one interactive elementTwo if you can defend the second. Before building it, write the reader's question it answers as a comment in the code. If a static chart answers that question, ship the static chart.
  6. A limits section with at least three real entries"Further research is needed" is not an entry. Name the confound, the survivor bias, the small cells.
  7. It works on a phone and in the darkSomeone will open it on a phone in a hospital corridor. The starter handles both; don't break them.
  8. Every quote and number traces to a sourceQuotes carry their speaker ID, figures name their file and their n. Nothing invented, no quotation tidied into something the person didn't say. If an assistant produced a number you can't find in the file, it isn't a number.
  9. Evidence, interpretation and proposal are visibly different thingsWhat the data says, what you think it means, and what you want them to do must not blur. A partner has to be able to disagree with your reading while still accepting your evidence.

The diagnostic, for when a team asks if theirs is good

Screenshot each section and paste the screenshots into a slide deck. If nothing is lost, they built a deck in HTML. Something has to depend on it being a page — the continuity of the argument, the one interaction, the fact that it has a URL.

What comes out of it

Three different outputs, worth separating

The artifact

One published page per team, live at a URL, finished as homework: five to seven claims in order, three or four charts, one interactive element, a limits section, study parameters above the fold, and an answer to whether the second source changed the argument. A 1,200–2,000 word read, in a single HTML file they keep and reuse for the rest of the quarter. Ungraded, or complete/incomplete — grading a first attempt at an unfamiliar form suppresses the risk-taking the second attempt needs.

The paper trail, which is the real assessment

Both come out of the session itself, and most workshops throw them away. The card spines — written before any screen opened, with one card physically removed at 5:35. They are the cleanest evidence you will get of whether a team can think in claims, separate from whether they can build, and in a single-session version they are the main thing you actually watched happen. Collect them.

And roughly eighty cold-read cards, where question 1 is a direct measure of whether the argument survived a stranger, written by readers rather than by you. If four readers wrote four different sentences, the team knows what happened without you saying anything.

Downstream — where the rubric actually applies

Each team takes one finding from their own partner research through the method, uses the readout in a real partner conversation, and submits the page plus: what the partner recognized, what they challenged and whether they were right, and what new question came out of it.

The readout is the instrument; what it produced in the room is the finding. A beautiful page that changed nothing is a weaker submission than a rough one that got a partner to say "that's not what's happening, it's this."

Before anything with partner data goes on a URL

Stated in class before week 7: unlisted link by default, nothing publicly indexed without the partner agreeing in writing. No attributable quotes — role and tenure only, never a name or a combination that identifies one person on one unit. And the partner sees it before anyone else does. A readout is more durable and more forwardable than a deck, which is its value and also its risk.

And the scheduling one: if the session runs hot, the minutes come out of the 5:53 build. Never out of the spine or the gallery — those two blocks are the session.

If you can borrow thirty minutes later

The one upgrade worth asking the calendar for

You do not need a second full block. You need thirty minutes at the top of any later session, once the readouts are in, to run the gallery again — this time on the finished pages.

MinBlock
20Silent gallery, built pages. Readouts open on screens, nobody at their own, four rotations of five minutes, cards. Questions become: what is it claiming · which number do you remember · where did you stop reading.
10Read the cards, and the two reveals. If nobody caught that the assistant cohort is also the newest cohort, put the hire-quarter cut on screen now. Then ask whether they would show the readout to the people it describes.

Question 3 on the card — where did you stop reading — is the one that earns the thirty minutes. "I liked the visuals" has never located a problem; "I stopped at section 04" locates it exactly, and usually two or three teams share the same section.

And if you later get a full second block

Split it back into the original two-session shape: session one ends after the spine gallery, and session two opens with the live build, gives teams a protected 34-minute build in named director/checker roles, and closes with the finished-page gallery. That version trades homework for supervised building, which helps the least technical teams most. It is better. It is not better enough to be worth displacing something else in a ten-week quarter, which is why this guide leads with the single session.
