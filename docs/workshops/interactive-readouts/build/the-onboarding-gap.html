The Onboarding Gap

Our orientation program is working. New hires rate it 4.2 out of 5. They also tell us that the thing they most needed to learn — how this place actually works — they mostly learned by getting it wrong in front of someone. Those two facts are not in tension. They are a description of what onboarding currently is.

The written layer of this organization transmits cleanly. The unwritten layer does not transmit at all.

We asked new hires seven questions about their first month. They split into two groups so cleanly that we have stopped treating them as one survey.

Everything a new hire can be told — the policy, the escalation path, where the equipment is — scores around four. Everything a new hire has to be inducted into — who to ask, what the real rules are, whether you belong here — scores around three. The program is not failing. It is succeeding at a narrower task than we thought we had assigned it.

Orientation was genuinely good. I could tell you the badge policy, the escalation chain, where to file an incident report. Then I walked onto the unit and had no idea when it was okay to interrupt a charge nurse. Nobody covers that.

One informal thing predicts a new hire's first three months better than anything we run on purpose.

We asked: in your first week, did someone informally show you how things really work here? Half said yes. That single yes/no is the strongest signal in the dataset — stronger than how highly someone rated orientation, stronger than whether their equipment arrived on time.

The half of the workforce that misses out is not a random half.

If the week-one guide were distributed by chance, this section would be short. It is not distributed by chance. It follows the shape of who happens to be standing near someone else — which means it reaches the people already closest to the centre of the organization, and misses the people at its edges.

Nursing does best, at two in three — not because nursing onboarding is better designed, but because units run a buddy arrangement that nobody has ever written down. Physicians do worst, at roughly one in five: they are assumed to arrive already knowing how organizations work. Night shift and hybrid hires sit well below the line for the same structural reason, which is that the people who could have shown them anything were not there.

My shift starts at ten at night. Everyone who could have shown me anything had gone home nine hours earlier. The night has its own rules and nobody has ever written them down, so you inherit them from whoever is willing to talk.

The people with the largest networks are the people least sure who to ask.

Everything above this point is self-report. Alongside it we pulled what the systems already record — scheduling, the paging directory, the learning platform, and the Ridge Assist logs — for the 131 of our 148 respondents who sit on the same systems.

Start with where the two sources agree, because it is what makes the rest readable. People who told us they had a week-one guide averaged 6.6 paired shifts in their first ten; people who told us they did not averaged 1.5. The survey question and the scheduling system are measuring the same thing, and they concur.

Then look at what happens when we put felt access next to actual contact.

Physicians contact roughly twice as many people as the average new hire and are the least likely of anyone here to say they knew who to ask. Support Services are the exact inverse — the smallest network in the organization and the highest confidence in it. Across the six groups the two measures are close to uncorrelated, and at the extremes they run backwards.

The reading we would defend: contact volume is not access. A physician is paged by thirty people in a month and has no one to bring a problem to. A housekeeping supervisor knows four people and one of them will tell her anything. What onboarding has to produce is not a wider network — it is one relationship that can carry a stupid question.

Where this data came from, and whether we should be using it

Two of these columns are paging metadata and shift records. Cedar Ridge collected them to run schedules and bill correctly, and we have used them here to assess whether people feel like they belong. We think the finding justifies it and we are not certain. At minimum, the 131 people in this section should be told that this is what their badge data was used for — and it is worth asking what a contact count will mean once people know someone is counting.

Attendings are assumed to arrive fully formed. You get credentialing and a parking code. The social layer you are expected to already have, which is a strange assumption to make about someone who has never been in the building.

Orientation is the one channel we fully control, and it is where almost nobody learns the rules that matter.

We asked people where they actually learned how things work here. Seven percent said orientation. Thirty percent said trial and error — which is not a channel, it is the absence of one.

Six months of policy content is delivered well to people who mostly needed to know which of the three shared drives is the real one, that the 7am huddle is optional in writing and mandatory in practice, and where people actually eat lunch. None of that is in the handbook. All of it is culture.

The handbook is 140 pages and it is accurate. That is the problem. Everything in it is true and none of it is what I needed on day four.

Ridge Assist answered the questions people used to have to ask a person. Fewer people then asked a person anything.

Ridge Assist rolled out across 2025, so 57 of our respondents onboarded with it and 91 did not. On the things it was built to do, it works: policy clarity is up, orientation is rated higher, day-one tools are marginally better. And the people who had it were meaningfully less likely to end up with a human they could go to.

Read this before you quote the last two rows

Ridge Assist rolled out over time, so everyone who had it is also a more recent hire. The assistant is confounded with recency, and with everything else that changed at Cedar Ridge in late 2025 — including a hiring surge that put more new people on units with fewer experienced ones. We cannot say the assistant caused the drop. We can say that the two arrived together, and that nothing in the rollout was designed to protect the relationship it partly replaced.

Ridge Assist is great, honestly. PTO policy, which form, who signs it — instant. It saved me a lot of small embarrassment.

[Later in the same interview] Ask me who I'd go to if I really messed something up. …Yeah. I don't know. I think I used up all my questions on the bot.

P13 · Admin & Ops · 5 months

The mechanism a colleague described plainly: it is free to ask the assistant a stupid question, and expensive to ask a person one. Every question routed to the assistant is a question that does not become a small, low-stakes contact with a human being — and those contacts were how people used to accumulate someone to go to.

It answers the questions that have answers. Most of what I needed to know didn't have an answer, it had an opinion, and it had to come from someone who had been here.

Stop trying to improve orientation. Design the informal layer on purpose.

Every instinct in the current program is to add content. The evidence says the content is fine and the transmission is missing. Four moves follow from that, ordered by how little permission they need.

  1. 01

    Name the Marisol role and put it on a schedule

    The buddy arrangement already exists on nursing units. It is invisible, unnamed, and therefore the first thing cut when a unit gets busy. Give it a name, four hours of protected time in week one, and a place on the roster — the same treatment any other real task gets.

    PracticesNorms
  2. 02

    Start with the groups below the line, not the average

    Night shift, hybrid hires, and physicians. The average hides all three, and each fails for a different structural reason: nobody present, nobody co-located, nobody who thinks they need it. Pilot three different arrangements, not one program.

    PolicyNorms
  3. 03

    Have Ridge Assist hand people to people

    The assistant is good and should stay. It is also, on the logs, a terminal endpoint — three handoffs to a named person across 1,103 queries. Every answer it gives about how something works can name the person on this unit who knows, and the fifth question from any new hire can route to a human rather than a paragraph.

    ArtifactsPractices
  4. 04

    Change what leadership sees

    Orientation completion sits at 97%, so from the executive floor onboarding is solved. Report the week-one guide rate beside it, cut by shift and work mode. A measure that is not reported is a measure nobody is accountable for.

    Metrics

We used to have a buddy system and it was informal, so when we got busy it was the first thing to go. Nobody decided to stop doing it.

Method and limits

A survey of 148 employees hired in the previous 18 months, fielded March 2026 against a sampling frame of 239 for a 62% response rate. Five-point agreement scales unless noted; weeks to productive is self-reported. Eighteen semi-structured interviews with recent hires and four with managers who onboard, 45–60 minutes, February–March 2026. Fielded by the People Experience team. Alongside the survey we joined scheduling, paging-directory, learning-platform and Ridge Assist records for the 131 respondents who sit on those systems; 17 per-diem and agency staff have no systems record and are excluded from section 04.

Five reasons to hold these findings loosely
  1. Self-report throughout. Weeks-to-productive is a memory, not a measurement, and people who are happy now remember ramping faster than they did.
  2. Survivor bias. Everyone in this sample still works here. The people whose onboarding failed worst are, by construction, missing from it.
  3. The assistant is confounded with recency. See the caution in section 06. Nothing here supports a causal claim about Ridge Assist.
  4. Fielded internally. Respondents knew the People Experience team would read their answers, which is not a neutral condition for a question about your manager.
  5. Two sources, one of which we did not collect for this purpose. The systems data was gathered to run schedules and bill correctly. Using it to assess belonging is a decision, not a neutral act, and the people it describes have not been told.
  6. Small cells. Physicians n = 14, Night n = 19, Remote n = 17. Cut twice and you are describing six people.

The survey instrument and the anonymized response file are available on request from People Experience.

Note for DESIGN 276

This page is a specimen, not a template to copy. It makes six claims, in order, and each one is a sentence you could read on its own and still follow the argument — scroll past the charts and read only the large type to test that. The things worth stealing are the structural ones: the study parameters visible above the fold, the claim carried in the heading rather than the chart title, the axis note under every figure, the confound flagged in the section that would otherwise be the most quotable, and the limits written as a list of five rather than a sentence saying "further research is needed."
