# Facilitation notes — running the readout workshop

The run of show is in `README.md`. This is the version for the person standing
in the room: what you say, what you hand out, what you are watching for, and
where you intervene.

One session, 110 minutes. Assumes 18–24 students in 5–6 teams of 3–4;
adjustments for other sizes at the end.

---

## The week before

**The session before — assign the pre-work (2 minutes).** With one session this
is not optional homework: the in-class teardown block is gone and the 4:40
contract harvest is built entirely on these sheets.

Say it roughly like this:

> Next week we stop making decks. Before then: read these three research
> pieces, at least fifteen minutes each. Fill in the teardown sheet for one of
> them — you'll be assigned which. Bring it on paper. Do not start building
> anything. If you find yourself opening a text editor, you have misread the
> assignment.

Assign the specimens **deliberately, not by choice** — split the room in three
so all three are in the room when you harvest the contract. Tape the assignment
to the teardown sheet so nobody has to remember.

**Your own prep — 40 minutes, and not on the day:**

1. Open the specimen readout and read it the way a student will. You need to be
   able to point at any section from memory.
2. Get last year's deck cleared. Email the team whose deck you want to use for
   the opener and ask permission. Use a **good** deck — the demonstration only
   works if nobody can dismiss it as a bad one.
2b. Decide which claim you will build live at 5:45, and make sure it is one no
   team will be working on. Build it once beforehand so the eight minutes are
   about method, not about you reading error messages.
3. Put `starter/readout-starter.html` and the `data/` folder somewhere students
   download in one click. Test the link from a phone.
4. Print: teardown sheets (one each), the Cedar Ridge methodology note and
   interview excerpts (one packet per team), cold-read cards (four per student).
5. Decide how teams will publish and test it end to end yourself. See "The URL
   problem" below. The 5:53 block depends on it taking ninety seconds per team.

## The session, block by block

### 4:30 · Cold read, on ourselves (10 min)

Do not explain the workshop. Start with the demonstration.

Put last year's deck on screen. Say only: *"Ninety seconds. Read this the way
you'd read it if it landed in your inbox."* Scroll at a natural pace. No
narration — harder than it sounds, because your instinct will be to say "so what
they found here was—". Say nothing.

Close it. *"Write down, in one sentence, what that deck claimed."* Collect four,
read them aloud without comment. They will not match. Then the thesis, once:

> That deck is good. The people who made it knew exactly what it claimed. The
> claim was in the room, in their voices, and it did not survive the email. For
> the rest of the quarter we are making things that survive the email.

**Watch for:** letting this run to fifteen minutes because the discussion is
interesting. With one session you cannot afford it. Cut it.

### 4:40 · Harvest the contract (10 min)

At the wall, straight off the pre-work sheets. You are collecting, not teaching.
Write what they say, in their words.

They reliably miss three. Fish for these:

- *"How many interactive things were in the Anthropic piece?"* → restraint
- *"What happens when you open one of these on your phone in a corridor?"*
- *"Where's the part that tells you why you might be wrong?"* → limits

Put the nine-item contract up beside their list and leave both there.

**If the pre-work didn't happen,** this block collapses — there is no in-class
teardown to fall back on. Chase it the week before and say why it matters.

### 4:50 · The claim spine — no screens (25 min)

Evidence packet on paper — methodology note and interview excerpts print well,
the CSV stays digital. Index cards.

> Five to seven claims. One per card. Each a full sentence with a verb, that
> you'd be willing to have argued with. Put them in the order you'd want someone
> to read them. No charts, no laptops, no formatting.

**This is the block, and the failure mode is you.** Around 4:58 a team will say
they think better on a screen, or that they just need to check one number. The
answer is no. With one session there is no second chance to recover the argument
if you lose it here.

Circulate for cards with no verb. "AI and onboarding." "The night shift
problem." Point and say *"that's a topic, give me the sentence."* You will say
this fifteen times. That is the job.

### 5:15 · The silent spine gallery (20 min)

Card stacks on tables around the room, cold-read cards beside each. Nobody at
their own table. Four rotations, four minutes, silence.

```
Spine: ____________________     Reader: ____________

1. In one sentence, what is this readout claiming?

2. Which claim is doing the least work?

3. Where does the argument jump?
```

Enforce the silence twice in the first five minutes and then it holds. Authors
will drift toward their own tables; move them.

This works better on spines than on finished pages — there is nothing to hide a
weak argument behind. Say that out loud when you set it up, or a team will read
the exercise as a rehearsal rather than the real critique.

### 5:35 · Read your cards, then cut one (10 min)

Three minutes silent. Then each team, under a minute: *"Readers thought we were
claiming ___. We meant ___."* The cards are more credible to them than you are;
resist adding.

Then every team physically removes one card. Seven claims is a team that has not
decided.

### 5:45 · Live build (8 min)

Laptops open. Build a section on the room screen with an assistant, out loud.
The point is watching you work, including where it goes wrong:

1. Say the claim as a sentence before typing anything.
2. Paste the contract into the prompt, not a vague ask.
3. **Check one number against the source, find it wrong, fix it.** If the
   assistant gets everything right, check anyway and narrate what you looked
   for.

Use a claim no team is working on — the orientation-completion one is safe.
This block exists to make the homework feel possible, so demonstrate the whole
loop badly and fast rather than one part of it well. Do not run past ten
minutes.

### 5:53 · Build your first section (17 min)

One section: claim, one chart, one quote, one axis note. Nobody finishes and
nobody is meant to. The purpose is that every team hits its first real obstacle
while you are still in the room.

Circulate fast and shallow — unblock, do not critique. The question stays *"show
me the sentence that says that."*

**Every team publishes before they leave**, however unfinished. This is the
single highest-leverage thing in the block: an unfinished page at a URL gets
finished, a blank file does not get started.

### 6:10 · Close, and hand out the complication (10 min)

Give the homework brief verbally as well as on paper. The second data source
needs one sentence of framing or teams read it as extra work:

> Your spine came from what people said. This is what the systems recorded.
> Does it change your argument?

Name the **checker** role before they go: one person per team owns every number
against its source file and every quote against its speaker ID. Nobody
volunteers for this and everyone assumes someone else is doing it.

## When the readouts come back

Two things you held back, worth ten minutes of any later session.

**The confound.** If nobody noticed that the assistant cohort is also the newest
cohort, put the hire-quarter cut next to the AI cut on screen, say nothing, and
let the room work out what it does to three of the readouts.

**The ethics question.** Two columns in the systems file are paging metadata and
shift records, collected to run schedules. Everyone just used them to decide
whether people feel like they belong, and nobody told the 131 people.

> Would you show this readout to the people it describes?

You are not looking for an answer. You are looking for the recognition that
choosing what to measure is itself a culture intervention — force eight, which
they performed on 131 people without discussing it first.

## The URL problem

The one piece of logistics that can eat the gallery. Decide Monday.

Decide before the session how teams will publish, and test it yourself. The
whole of the 5:53 block depends on it working in about ninety seconds per team.

**Publish unfinished.** Every team gets a URL before they leave, whatever state
the page is in. They republish to the same address as they work on it at home.
An unfinished page at a URL gets finished; a blank file does not get started.

If publishing fails for one team, do not spend the block fixing it — send them
home with the local file and sort it by email. Nothing in this session's
critique depends on a URL, because the gallery runs on index cards.

## The four moments you actually have to intervene

Everything else runs itself. These don't:

1. **4:58** — the team that wants to open a laptop during the spine block. Say
   no. This is the entire pedagogical bet, and in a single session there is no
   recovering it later.
2. **5:20** — the author explaining their spine in the gallery. Move them
   physically. One exception granted and the block is over.
3. **5:53** — the team still arguing about the claim instead of building. Let
   them. A team that leaves with a tested argument and no page is in better
   shape than the reverse; the page is homework, the argument is not.
4. **Whenever a team says a number an assistant gave them** and cannot point at
   the row it came from. Stop and make them find it. The first time you do this
   the room learns that rule 8 is real; if you let one through, it isn't.

## If you later get a second block

Split it back into the original two-session shape: session one ends after the
spine gallery, and session two opens with the live build, gives teams a
protected 34-minute build in named director/checker roles, and closes with the
gallery on finished pages. That trades homework for supervised building, which
helps the least technical teams most.

Short of a full block, thirty minutes at the top of any later session buys the
finished-page gallery — twenty minutes of silent reading with the "where did you
stop reading" card, then ten for the cards and the two reveals above.

## Sizing

- **Fewer than 4 teams:** the spine gallery gets short. Have each person read
  *every* spine, more minutes each, and skip the rotation timer.
- **More than 6 teams:** don't extend the gallery past twenty minutes. Split the
  room in two halves that read within their half, then pair one team from each
  half at 5:35 so the cross-talk still happens.
- **Teams of 5+:** the spine block goes slack — two people write and three
  watch. Split into pairs within the team, write two competing spines, and make
  them merge before laptops open. That's a better block anyway.
