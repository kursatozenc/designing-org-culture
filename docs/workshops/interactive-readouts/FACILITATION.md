# Facilitation notes — running the readout workshop

The run of show is in `README.md`. This is the version for the person standing
in the room: what you say, what you hand out, what you are watching for, and
where you intervene.

Assumes 18–24 students in 5–6 teams of 3–4. Adjustments for other sizes at the
end.

---

## The week before

**Thursday, end of class — assign the pre-work (2 minutes).**

Say it roughly like this:

> Next week we stop making decks. Before Tuesday: read these three research
> pieces, at least fifteen minutes each. Fill in the teardown sheet for one of
> them — you'll be assigned which. Bring it on paper. Do not start building
> anything. If you find yourself opening a text editor, you have misread the
> assignment.

Assign the specimens **deliberately, not by choice**. Split the room in three so
that in Tuesday's pair block, people are trading with someone who read something
different. Tape the assignment to the teardown sheet so nobody has to remember.

**Your own prep — 30 minutes, do it Monday not Tuesday afternoon:**

1. Open the specimen readout and read it the way a student will. You need to be
   able to point at any section from memory.
2. Get last year's deck cleared. Email the team whose deck you want to use for
   the opener and ask permission. Use a **good** deck — the demonstration only
   works if nobody can dismiss it as a bad one.
3. Put `starter/readout-starter.html` and the `data/` folder somewhere students
   download in one click. Test the link from a phone.
4. Print: teardown sheets (one each), the Cedar Ridge methodology note and
   interview excerpts (one packet per team), cold-read cards (four per student).
5. Decide how teams will publish. See "The URL problem" below. Decide Monday.
   Not at 5:18 on Thursday.

---

## Tuesday — Read one, take it apart

### 4:30 · Cold read, on ourselves (10 min)

Do not explain the workshop first. Start with the demonstration.

Put last year's deck on screen. Say only: *"Ninety seconds. Read this the way
you'd read it if it landed in your inbox."* Scroll it at a natural pace. No
narration — and this is harder than it sounds, because your instinct will be to
say "so what they found here was—". Say nothing.

Close it. Then: *"Write down, in one sentence, what that deck claimed."*

Collect four and read them aloud without comment. They will not match. Some will
be topics rather than claims ("it was about onboarding"). Someone will have
written down a number from a slide they liked.

Then say the thesis, once, and move on:

> That deck is good. The people who made it knew exactly what it claimed. The
> claim was in the room, in their voices, and it did not survive the email. For
> the rest of the quarter we are making things that survive the email.

**Watch for:** the temptation to let this run to fifteen minutes because the
discussion is interesting. It is a demonstration, not a discussion. Cut it.

### 4:40 · Teardown, in pairs (20 min)

Pairs who read **different** specimens. They trade sheets and interrogate each
other's answers to Q1–Q3 — especially Q2, *where in the piece is the claim
actually written down*.

You are circulating with one question: *"You both wrote different sentences for
question one. Which of you is wrong?"* Usually neither, and the answer is that
the piece carries two claims and never decides which is primary. That is the
insight; let them reach it.

### 5:00 · Build the contract (15 min)

Whole room, standing at the wall. Harvest the teardowns into a list of what a
readout has to do. Write what they say, in their words.

They will reliably produce: the argument has to be written down, you need the
sample size, don't make me scroll to find the point. They reliably **miss**
three, and you should fish for these:

- *"How many interactive things were in the Anthropic piece?"* → restraint
- *"What happens when you open one of these on your phone in a corridor?"*
- *"Where's the part that tells you why you might be wrong?"* → limits

Land on the seven-item contract from the README. Leave it on the wall for
Thursday — do not take it down, do not retype it into a slide.

### 5:15 · The claim spine — no screens (20 min)

Hand out the Cedar Ridge packets and the index cards. The instruction:

> Five to seven claims. One per card. Each one a full sentence with a verb, that
> you'd be willing to have argued with. Put them in the order you'd want someone
> to read them. No charts, no laptops, no formatting. Twenty minutes.

**This is the block that makes or breaks the workshop, and the failure mode is
you.** Somewhere around 5:22 a team will say they think better on a screen, or
that they just need to check one number. The answer is no. Twenty minutes of
writing sentences you cannot style is the only reliable way to get an argument
before an aesthetic. Hold it.

What you're circulating for: cards with no verb. "AI and onboarding." "The
night shift problem." Point at it and say *"that's a topic, give me the
sentence."* You will say this fifteen times in twenty minutes. That is the job.

### 5:35 · Spine swap (10 min)

Teams trade card stacks. Read a stranger's spine cold and write on the back of
the last card: what is this readout about, and where does the argument jump?
Hand it back. No discussion between teams — the note has to stand alone, same as
the readout will.

### 5:45 · First section only (25 min)

Laptops open. Open the starter. Build **one** section: claim, one chart, one
quote, one axis note. Nothing else.

Say the constraint twice, because half the room will try to build the whole
thing. The point of one section is that a team that builds one good section on
Tuesday knows what they're doing on Thursday; a team that builds six rough ones
knows nothing.

**Circulate with exactly one question:** *"Show me the sentence that says
that."* Whatever they point at on screen, whatever they explain to you — ask
where it is written. If it's only in their mouths, it isn't in the readout.

**Get every team publishing now, not Thursday.** Have them publish the
half-finished section and get a URL before they leave. See below.

### 6:10 · Close (10 min)

One sentence per team: the claim they are **cutting** before Thursday. Cutting,
not adding. Every team has seven claims and needs five.

---

## Between Tuesday and Thursday

Nothing assigned. Teams will work anyway; that's fine, but don't require it —
the Thursday build block has to be real work in the room or the gallery becomes
a demo of homework.

**Your one job:** open every team's URL Wednesday night and read it for ninety
seconds each. Write nothing to them. You are finding out which teams are going
to be in trouble at 5:15 Thursday, so you know where to stand.

---

## Thursday — Build it, then be silent while it's read

### 4:30 · Constraints back up (5 min)

Point at the wall. Read item 5 out loud — exactly one interactive element. Then:

> By 5:20 every one of these is on a screen and nobody is standing next to
> their own. You will not get to explain anything. Build accordingly.

Naming the gallery at 4:30 changes what gets built for the next forty minutes.
Do not skip this.

### 4:35 · Build (40 min)

Teams build the full draft. You and the TA circulate, still with *"show me the
sentence that says that."*

Second question to add today, for teams that look finished: *"Read me the
headings, in order, and nothing else."* If it doesn't hold as an argument, they
have a decorated document.

**The team to stand next to** is the one whose page looks best at 5:00. A
beautiful page with no argument is the most likely failure in the room and it
disguises itself as being ahead.

### 5:15 · Ship it (5 min)

Everything goes to its final published state. Hard stop at 5:20 regardless of
whether teams are finished. Say this at 4:35 so it isn't a surprise.

A half-finished readout being cold-read teaches more than a finished one nobody
reads.

### 5:20 · The silent gallery (30 min)

Six screens around the room, each showing one team's readout, each with a stack
of cold-read cards beside it. **Nobody stands at their own screen.**

Four rotations, six minutes each, plus movement. Silence throughout — you will
have to enforce this twice in the first five minutes, then it holds.

The card, printed:

```
Readout: ____________________     Reader: ____________

1. In one sentence, what is this readout claiming?

2. Which single number do you remember?

3. Where did you stop reading? Name the section.
```

Question 3 does the work. "I really liked the visuals" has never located a
problem; "I stopped at section 04" locates it exactly.

**Your job during this block is to be a bouncer.** Authors will drift toward
their own screens. People will start talking. Move them, quietly, and keep the
silence — the silence *is* the condition you are simulating.

### 5:50 · Read your cards (20 min)

Teams return to their own screen and read their stack in silence for three
minutes. Then each team says out loud, in under a minute:

> Readers thought we were claiming ___. We meant ___.

That gap is the whole critique. You barely have to add anything, and you should
resist — the cards are more credible to them than you are.

Go round the room. Then one question to the whole room: *"Whose cards said they
stopped in the same place?"* Usually two or three teams share a failure, and it
is usually the third section, where every readout sags.

### 6:10 · Close (10 min)

One named change per team before a partner sees anything in this format.

**If nobody found the confound**, this is where you put it on screen. Show the
hire-quarter cut next to the AI cut, say nothing for a moment, and let the room
work out what it does to three of the readouts they just read. Do not do this
earlier — handing it to them during the build costs the lesson entirely.

## The URL problem

The one piece of logistics that can eat the gallery. Decide Monday.

**Publish early, not at the end.** Have every team publish a nearly-empty page
in Tuesday's build block and get a URL then. They republish to the same address
as they work. This removes the 5:20 cliff completely — at 5:20 the page is
already live, whatever state it's in.

**The fallback is laptops.** If publishing fails for a team, the gallery works
perfectly well with their file open on their own laptop, left on the table while
they walk away. Do not let a publishing problem threaten the gallery.

I said in the README that a local file isn't done. For the *deliverable* that
holds — a readout without an address is a deck with extra steps, and the partner
needs a link. For the workshop gallery, it does not. Run the gallery.

---

## The three moments you actually have to intervene

Everything else runs itself. These don't:

1. **5:22 Tuesday** — the team that wants to open a laptop during the spine
   block. Say no. This is the entire pedagogical bet of the workshop.
2. **5:00 Thursday** — the beautiful empty page. Make them read you the
   headings in order. If it doesn't hold, they close the laptop and go back to
   cards while everyone else keeps building. Losing twenty minutes of build is
   cheaper than shipping a decorated void.
3. **5:25 Thursday** — the author explaining their work in the gallery. Move
   them physically. One exception granted and the block is over.

## If you only have one session

Cut to 110 minutes, in this order, and lose the build:

| Time | Min | Block |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 4:30 | 10 | Cold read on last year's deck |
| 4:40 | 15 | Teardown in pairs |
| 4:55 | 10 | Build the contract |
| 5:05 | 25 | Claim spine on cards |
| 5:30 | 15 | Spine swap, read cold |
| 5:45 | 25 | Build one section only |
| 6:10 | 10 | Close |

You keep the argument, which is the transferable part, and you lose the gallery,
which is the memorable part. Assign the full readout as homework and run the
gallery as the first 30 minutes of the next session.

Do not cut the spine block to make room for more building. That inverts the
whole workshop.

---

## Sizing

- **Fewer than 4 teams:** the gallery gets short. Have each person read *every*
  readout, more minutes each, and skip the rotation timer.
- **More than 6 teams:** don't extend the gallery. Split the room in two halves
  that read within their half, then pair one team from each half for the 5:50
  block so the cross-talk still happens.
- **Teams of 5+:** the spine block goes slack — two people write and three
  watch. Split into pairs within the team, write two competing spines, and make
  them merge before laptops open. That's a better block anyway.
